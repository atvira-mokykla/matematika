Solving by completing the square

Four moves that turn any quadratic equation into a square equal to a number.

PUPPCommon coreLevels 1–3 · threshold to basicAbout 45 min · 1 class periodProgramme 32.1.1.1, 31.2.1
PDF
Handout lesson summary, examplesWorksheet check items, exam taskAnswer key answers, marking, teacher notes
Goal

In this lesson

Rewrite any quadratic equation as (x+p)2=d(x + p)^2 = d(x+p)2=d and solve it.

By the end of the lesson

  • I can rewrite x² + bx + c as (x + b/2)² + q, also when b/2 is a fraction.
  • I can solve a quadratic equation by completing the square, and tell from (x + p)² = d whether it has two roots, one root or none.

What you need from before

  • Lesson 1.1 · Quadratic equations: what we can already solve
  • Operations with fractions
  • Completing the square in simple cases

This time we knew which square to aim for. How do you find the right square for an equation you have never seen, like x2+6x−7=0x^2 + 6x - 7 = 0x2+6x−7=0?

Explain

Completing the square

A square and a strip

Take x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x. Draw x2x^2x2 as a square with side xxx, and 6x6x6x as a strip that is 666 wide and xxx long.

Split the strip

Cut the strip into two strips, each 3×x3 \times x3×x. Put one beside the square and one below it. The area is still x2+6xx^2 + 6xx2+6x.

The 333 is half of 666.

Fill the corner

One 3×33 \times 33×3 corner is missing. Adding 32=93^2 = 932=9 completes a square with side x+3x + 3x+3:

x2+6x+9=(x+3)2.x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2.x2+6x+9=(x+3)2.

The drawing only works for x>0x > 0x>0, but the identity holds for every xxx: multiply out (x+3)2(x + 3)^2(x+3)2 and you get x2+6x+9x^2 + 6x + 9x2+6x+9.

This is called completing the square.

The rule

For any number bbb, halve it and square the half:

x2+bx+(b2)2=(x+b2)2.x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2.x2+bx+(2b​)2=(x+2b​)2.

Check it by multiplying out the right side:

(x+b2)2=x2+2⋅x⋅b2+(b2)2=x2+bx+(b2)2.\begin{aligned} \left(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 &= x^2 + 2 \cdot x \cdot \tfrac{b}{2} + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 \\ &= x^2 + bx + \left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2. \end{aligned}(x+2b​)2​=x2+2⋅x⋅2b​+(2b​)2=x2+bx+(2b​)2.​
  • bbb negative: x2−4x+4=(x−2)2x^2 - 4x + 4 = (x - 2)^2x2−4x+4=(x−2)2. Half of −4-4−4 is −2-2−2. The number added is its square, (−2)2=4(-2)^2 = 4(−2)2=4, which is positive. (The drawing has no strips of negative width, but the algebra works.)
  • bbb odd: x2+x+14=(x+12)2.x^2 + x + \frac{1}{4} = \left(x + \frac{1}{2}\right)^2.x2+x+41​=(x+21​)2. Fractions are normal here.

A trinomial with any constant term becomes a square plus a number. Add (b2)2\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 and take it away again:

x2+6x+5=x2+6x+9⏟(x+3)2−9+5=(x+3)2−4.\begin{aligned} x^2 + 6x + 5 &= \underbrace{x^2 + 6x + 9}_{(x + 3)^2} - 9 + 5 \\ &= (x + 3)^2 - 4. \end{aligned}x2+6x+5​=(x+3)2x2+6x+9​​−9+5=(x+3)2−4.​

The four moves for ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0:

  1. If a≠1a \ne 1a=1, divide both sides by aaa. This is allowed because a≠0a \ne 0a=0, and it does not change the roots.
  2. Move the constant term to the right side.
  3. Add (b2)2\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to both sides, where bbb is now the coefficient of xxx.
  4. Write the left side as a square, (x+p)2=d(x + p)^2 = d(x+p)2=d, and solve it as in lesson 1.1.

Two roots, one, or none

Move 4 ends with (x+p)2=d(x + p)^2 = d(x+p)2=d, and ddd decides, as in lesson 1.1:

  • d>0d > 0d>0.   x2−2x−1=0\;x^2 - 2x - 1 = 0x2−2x−1=0 becomes (x−1)2=2(x - 1)^2 = 2(x−1)2=2. So x−1=2x - 1 = \sqrt{2}x−1=2​ or x−1=−2x - 1 = -\sqrt{2}x−1=−2​: two roots, x=1+2x = 1 + \sqrt{2}x=1+2​ and x=1−2x = 1 - \sqrt{2}x=1−2​. No amount of guessing whole numbers would find them.
  • d=0d = 0d=0.   x2−6x+9=0\;x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0x2−6x+9=0 becomes (x−3)2=0(x - 3)^2 = 0(x−3)2=0: one root, x=3x = 3x=3.
  • d<0d < 0d<0.   x2+2x+5=0\;x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0x2+2x+5=0 becomes (x+1)2=−4(x + 1)^2 = -4(x+1)2=−4: no real roots.

Two roots are often written together with the sign ±\pm± (“plus or minus”): x=1±2x = 1 \pm \sqrt{2}x=1±2​ means x=1+2x = 1 + \sqrt{2}x=1+2​ or x=1−2x = 1 - \sqrt{2}x=1−2​.

Teacher notes

For the teacher

Pupils can say: “I can complete the square in x2+bx+cx^2 + bx + cx2+bx+c, also when bbb is odd; I can solve a quadratic equation by completing the square, and tell from (x+p)2=d(x + p)^2 = d(x+p)2=d whether it has two roots, one or none.”

Where this lesson sits. Grade 8 (31.2.1) completed the square “in simple cases” as an identity. Here it becomes a method for solving any quadratic equation, including odd bbb and a≠1a \ne 1a=1. The hook runs lesson 1.1’s (D) → (E) backwards. The four moves are exactly the ones lesson 1.3 makes once with letters to prove the quadratic formula, so name them the same way in class.

Common mistakes and what helps

  • Adding (b2)2\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 to one side only (c2). Say “both sides” every time, and write the added number on both sides in the same colour.
  • Not halving bbb, or squaring bbb itself (c1, c2, c3). Ask “what is the number in the bracket?” before squaring.
  • Sign slips with negative bbb: half of −8-8−8 is −4-4−4, but the number added is (−4)2=16(-4)^2 = 16(−4)2=16 (c1). The bracket keeps the sign: (x−4)2(x - 4)^2(x−4)2.
  • Forgetting to divide ccc by aaa as well (Example 3, c7): 4x2−8x+3=04x^2 - 8x + 3 = 04x2−8x+3=0 becomes x2−2x+34=0x^2 - 2x + \frac{3}{4} = 0x2−2x+43​=0, not x2−2x+3=0x^2 - 2x + 3 = 0x2−2x+3=0.
  • Fraction arithmetic: −4+254=94-4 + \frac{25}{4} = \frac{9}{4}−4+425​=49​. The Refresh box in Remember helps.
  • In (x+p)2+q(x + p)^2 + q(x+p)2+q, adding (b2)2\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2(2b​)2 without taking it away again (c3).
  • Forgetting the negative case after “take the root” (all Numeric items have a Why for this).
