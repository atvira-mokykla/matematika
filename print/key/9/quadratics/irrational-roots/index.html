Lesson 1.2’s method gives the same roots: x2−6x+7=0x^2 - 6x + 7 = 0x2−6x+7=0 becomes (x−3)2=2(x - 3)^2 = 2(x−3)2=2, so x=3±2x = 3 \pm \sqrt{2}x=3±2​.

When is it simple enough?

Roots like these are in their simplest form when

  1. no perfect-square factor is left under the root (8\sqrt{8}8​ becomes 222\sqrt{2}22​), and
  2. no whole number greater than 111 divides all three: the term without the root, the number in front of the root, and the denominator.

For example, in 6±234\dfrac{6 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{4}46±23​​ the numbers 666, 222 and 444 are all divisible by 222:

6±234=3±32.\frac{6 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{4} = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{3}}{2}.46±23​​=23±3​​.

But −3±1292\dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{129}}{2}2−3±129​​ stays as it is: 333, 111 and 222 have no common factor.

Both ways of writing the roots are fine: x=3±2x = 3 \pm \sqrt{2}x=3±2​, or x1=3−2x_1 = 3 - \sqrt{2}x1​=3−2​ and x2=3+2x_2 = 3 + \sqrt{2}x2​=3+2​. In the answer boxes on this site, type 2\sqrt{\phantom{2}}2​ as sqrt and ±\pm± as +-, e.g. 3 +- sqrt(2).

Summary

What to remember

  • When the coefficients are whole numbers and D>0D > 0D>0 is not a perfect square, the roots are irrational. Leave D\sqrt{D}D​ as a root: the roots are then exact.
  • Simplify D\sqrt{D}D​: find the largest perfect-square factor and take out its root, e.g. 20=4⋅5=25\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 5} = 2\sqrt{5}20​=4⋅5​=25​.
  • Divide the whole numerator: 4±252=2±5\dfrac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} = 2 \pm \sqrt{5}24±25​​=2±5​, not 2±252 \pm 2\sqrt{5}2±25​. Cancel the fraction only by a common factor of all three numbers: 6±234=3±32\dfrac{6 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{4} = \dfrac{3 \pm \sqrt{3}}{2}46±23​​=23±3​​.
  • Round at the very end, and only when the question asks for a decimal answer or a decimal value is useful.

Teacher notes

For the teacher

Pupils can say: “I can solve a quadratic equation whose roots are irrational, write the roots exactly and in their simplest form, and round them when that is asked for.”

Where this lesson sits. Lesson 1.3 used the formula only when DDD was a perfect square. Here the same formula meets any positive DDD. The new work is grade 8’s simplifying of roots (31.1.1) used inside the formula, and cancelling a common factor of the whole numerator and the denominator. The second garden from lesson 1.3 is solved in the first frame. The cases D=0D = 0D=0 and D<0D < 0D<0 are lesson 1.5.

Common mistakes and what helps

  • Dividing only one term of the numerator: 4±252=2±25\frac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} = 2 \pm 2\sqrt{5}24±25​​=2±25​ (t1, c1–c4). The numeric check 6+42=5\frac{6 + 4}{2} = 526+4​=5 convinces most pupils; so does taking the factor out first, 2(2±5)2\frac{2(2 \pm \sqrt{5})}{2}22(2±5​)​.
  • “Halving under the root”: 122=6\frac{\sqrt{12}}{2} = \sqrt{6}212​​=6​ (c1).
  • Cancelling the wrong factor when a≠1a \ne 1a=1: 6±234\frac{6 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{4}46±23​​ becomes 3±32\frac{3 \pm \sqrt{3}}{2}23±3​​, not 3±33 \pm \sqrt{3}3±3​ (Example 2, t2, c3). Ask “which number divides all three?”
  • Rounding too early: rounding 129\sqrt{129}129​ to 111111 gives a width of 444 m and an area of 282828 m², not 303030 m².
  • Taking D\sqrt{D}D​ out incompletely (32=28\sqrt{32} = 2\sqrt{8}32​=28​) (r1).
