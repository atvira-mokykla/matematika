Goal

In this lesson

Prove the quadratic formula and use it to solve equations whose discriminant is a perfect square.

By the end of the lesson

  • I can prove the quadratic formula by completing the square, explaining why each step is allowed.
  • I can solve a quadratic equation with integer coefficients by the formula when D is a perfect square, and check a root by substituting it.

Completing the square would work, but it means the same four moves again, with new fractions. In lesson 1.2 we made the same four moves every time, only with different numbers. Why each step is allowed:

  1. Divide by aaa. A quadratic equation has a≠0a \ne 0a=0, so we may divide both sides by aaa. The roots do not change.
  2. Move the constant term. Subtract ca\frac{c}{a}ac​ from both sides.
  3. Add the square of half the coefficient of xxx. The coefficient of xxx is now ba\frac{b}{a}ab​. Half of it is b2a\frac{b}{2a}2ab​, and its square is b24a2\frac{b^2}{4a^2}4a2b2​. Add it to both sides.
  4. Write the left side as a square. This is lesson 1.2’s rule, with ba\frac{b}{a}ab​ in place of bbb.

The quadratic formula

The roots of ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 (a≠0)(a \ne 0)(a=0) are

  x1,2=−b±D2a  \boxed{\;x_{1,2} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}\;}x1,2​=2a−b±D​​​

where D=b2−4acD = b^2 - 4acD=b2−4ac; the formula is used when D≥0D \ge 0D≥0. Written out, as on the PUPP formula sheet:

x1=−b−D2a,x2=−b+D2a.\begin{aligned} x_1 &= \frac{-b - \sqrt{D}}{2a}, \\[4pt] x_2 &= \frac{-b + \sqrt{D}}{2a}. \end{aligned}x1​x2​​=2a−b−D​​,=2a−b+D​​.​

How to use it:

  1. Write the equation as ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 and read aaa, bbb, ccc with their signs.
  2. Work out D=b2−4acD = b^2 - 4acD=b2−4ac. Put negative numbers in brackets: (−3)2=9(-3)^2 = 9(−3)2=9.
  3. Work out D\sqrt{D}D​. In this lesson DDD is always a perfect square, so D\sqrt{D}D​ is a whole number.
  4. Write x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​. The whole numerator −b±D-b \pm \sqrt{D}−b±D​ is divided by 2a2a2a.
  5. Check a root by substituting it into the equation.

Summary

What to remember

  • The discriminant of ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0 is D=b2−4acD = b^2 - 4acD=b2−4ac.
  • The quadratic formula: if D≥0D \ge 0D≥0, the roots are x1,2=−b±D2ax_{1,2} = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}x1,2​=2a−b±D​​ (x1x_1x1​ with the minus, x2x_2x2​ with the plus, as on the formula sheet).
  • It comes from the four moves of completing the square, made once with aaa, bbb and ccc. We may divide by aaa because a≠0a \ne 0a=0.
  • How to use it: write ax2+bx+c=0ax^2 + bx + c = 0ax2+bx+c=0; read aaa, bbb, ccc with their signs; work out DDD with brackets around negative numbers; divide the whole numerator by 2a2a2a; check a root by substituting it.
  • In a word problem, keep only the roots that make sense.

Teacher notes

For the teacher

Pupils can say: “I can explain where the quadratic formula comes from, step by step, and I can use it to solve an equation when the discriminant is a perfect square.”

Where this lesson sits. Lesson 1.2 made the four moves with numbers. Here the same moves are made once with letters. This is the proof the programme asks for (“Įrodoma ir taikoma…”). Name the moves exactly as in lesson 1.2. All the discriminants in this lesson are perfect squares. Irrational roots are lesson 1.4; the cases D=0D = 0D=0 and D<0D < 0D<0 are lesson 1.5.

The step with ∣a∣|a|∣a∣. 4a2=2∣a∣\sqrt{4a^2} = 2|a|4a2​=2∣a∣, and the ±\pm± makes the formula right for a<0a < 0a<0 as well. Most textbooks skip this step. Show it to strong pupils; for the others, “the ±\pm± covers both signs” is enough. A quick oral example: −x2+4x+5=0-x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0−x2+4x+5=0 (lesson 1.1’s (E) multiplied by −1-1−1) gives x1,2=−4±6−2x_{1,2} = \frac{-4 \pm 6}{-2}x1,2​=−2−4±6​, that is, 555 and −1-1−1, the same roots as (E).

Labels. The formula sheet labels the roots x1=−b−D2ax_1 = \frac{-b - \sqrt{D}}{2a}x1​=2a−b−D​​ and x2=−b+D2ax_2 = \frac{-b + \sqrt{D}}{2a}x2​=2a−b+D​​, and this lesson follows it. The NŠA marking keys of 2025 and 2026 label them the other way round. Which root is called x1x_1x1​ does not matter; both roots must be found.

Common mistakes and what helps

  • −b-b−b when bbb is negative: b=−3b = -3b=−3 gives −b=3-b = 3−b=3 (Example 2, t2, c2).
  • Squaring a negative number, (−7)2=49(-7)^2 = 49(−7)2=49, and the sign of −4ac-4ac−4ac (c1). Have pupils write every coefficient in brackets: D=(−3)2−4⋅2⋅(−2)D = (-3)^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2)D=(−3)2−4⋅2⋅(−2).
  • Dividing by 222 instead of 2a2a2a, or dividing only D\sqrt{D}D​ by 2a2a2a (t2, c2). Write the whole numerator over 2a2a2a first.
  • Not checking. A substitution catches a wrong root at once (c3: Tomas’s x=2x = 2x=2 gives 444, not 000).
  • Reading aaa, bbb, ccc before the equation has the form …=0\ldots = 0…=0 (r1; the garden’s x(x+3)=40x(x + 3) = 40x(x+3)=40).
  • Keeping a root that makes no sense in a word problem (Example 1, the exam task).
